Language is not a precise instrument for conveying intention. It is closer to a crude metaphor through which we approximate one another's intentions.
{: .article-lead }

A person turns a thought into language, and the listener turns that language back into a thought of their own. If this were an exact conversion, everyone who heard the same words would understand the same thing. Yet the scope of a word differs from person to person, and the meaning of a sentence changes with the relationship and the situation around it.

The problem is not only that people understand language differently. We do not fully understand our own intentions either. Sometimes we know exactly what we want and need only put it into words. Often, however, we begin speaking with nothing more than a vague discomfort or desire. Only after we say something, see another person's response, and encounter an unexpected result do we recognize what we wanted in the first place.

My own understanding of my intent is already an approximation. Turning it into language introduces another error, and the listener's interpretation through their own experience changes it once again. Communication is not the transfer of a finished intention from one mind to another. It is the work of gradually bringing different approximations closer together.

## A tower built of jelly {#jelly-tower}

People do not communicate through language alone.

The pace and pitch of a voice, facial expressions, eye contact, gestures, and silence all supplement the meaning of a sentence. Interpretation is also shaped by the relationship between two people, what they have experienced together, and where and why the conversation is taking place. The same words mean something different when spoken with a smile or with a rigid expression. Nor can “Do it the usual way” mean the same thing to a longtime colleague and to someone you have just met.

Communication is not like building a tower from squared bricks. It is closer to balancing a tower of slippery, rounded pieces of jelly, leaning them against one another just well enough to hold their shape for a while. The tower stands not because its materials are exact, but because the people in the conversation keep reaching out to steady it whenever it begins to sway.

Sending a sentence, then, does not mean that an intention has arrived. The speaker must adjust the expression, and the listener must infer what is missing. Each must check whether those inferences are right and rebuild when they are not. As long as communication continues, invisible corrections are taking place.

## Room to interpret {#room-to-interpret}

Communicating well requires the ability to speak clearly. But the effort to speak clearly is not enough. The other person will not always understand their own intent precisely or present it in a finished sentence.

When intelligence is roughly equal, the difference comes from how much attention one can give to another person's imperfect words. Someone with more emotional bandwidth can separate the literal meaning from the intention the speaker was trying to convey. They can leave open the possibility that their interpretation is wrong, look to the situation and the relationship for missing premises, and choose among several interpretations by considering the wider context.

Without that bandwidth, a sentence is more likely to be processed in its narrowest and most immediate sense. The rough surface of the expression draws the first reaction, and there may be no energy left to examine the intention underneath it. Even interest and goodwill cannot reduce the error in communication if there is no bandwidth left to spend on interpretation.

This does not mean that having more emotional bandwidth gives someone license to interpret another's words in whatever favorable light they please. Interpreting broadly does not mean selecting the meaning one wants to hear. It means refusing to declare one interpretation correct too quickly. Leaving something unresolved and asking again are also part of interpretation.

## Saying we understand {#claiming-understanding}

People often treat quick comprehension as a sign of communicative ability. Answering immediately after a single explanation looks clever; asking several times can look slow. But answering quickly and moving closer to another person's intention are not the same thing.

When even the speaker has not yet organized their thoughts, complete understanding in one attempt is nearly impossible. Saying “I understand” can then become a way of mistaking one's own interpretation for the speaker's intention. There may be no intent to lie, but the result is still a claim to understand what has not yet been understood.

Good communication appears less in the declaration of understanding than in showing what one has understood. When a listener reflects the meaning they received, distinguishes what seems certain from what remains unclear, the speaker can examine their own intention again. Confirmation is not waste that merely slows a conversation. It reduces the cost of returning to the beginning after two interpretations have traveled far apart.

The speaker's intention may also change in this process. While trying to point out that the other person's interpretation is wrong, the speaker may discover that they had no clear standard of their own. Communication not only conveys an intention that already exists. It can also be a process of finding an intention that has not yet taken shape.

## Language outside the community {#language-outside-community}

People rarely notice how much shared experience is attached to the words they use.

Within the same organization, profession, generation, or culture, much can be understood without being said. Someone who has worked with us often can supplement a short and ambiguous request with past examples and knowledge of our preferences. Expressions such as “Use your judgment,” “Do it the usual way,” or “Make it feel more natural” work as instructions not because the sentences are specific, but because the context already accumulated between two people fills in the blanks.

Outside the boundary of a community, familiar language suddenly becomes unfamiliar. Even among speakers of the same language, the meaning of a word can differ across organizations and generations. Sharing a dictionary definition is not the same as sharing the implications produced by living in the same world.

The difference grows in conversations with AI. An AI may know the general meaning of a word, but it cannot initially know which experiences and preferences that word points to for a particular user. Speaking in Korean does not mean sharing the whole texture of Korean life, and familiarity with Korean culture does not include the language habits of every organization or individual.

A premise so familiar to the user that it seems not worth mentioning may not exist for the AI at all. Conversely, the AI may assign an unexpected meaning to a word the user chose out of habit and without much thought. Looking at the same sentence does not mean thinking the same thought.

## An unfamiliar mirror {#unfamiliar-mirror}

AI is not a perfect mirror that reflects human language exactly. Its learned information and methods of interpretation have biases and limits, and it can produce a misguided result even when given ample context. Not every problem in an AI result can be blamed on the user's expression.

Even so, AI reflects the user's language in an unfamiliar way. Gaps that relationships and shared experience quietly filled in human conversation appear as awkwardness in the result. A word that worked with familiar people is interpreted more broadly than expected. A condition the user assumed was obviously included disappears. A preference that seemed clear turns out to provide no usable standard for judgment.

At that moment, the user sees both how the AI understood the words and what the user left unsaid. The result may feel unfamiliar because the AI's interpretation is unfamiliar. But it may also feel unfamiliar because it reveals the shape of the user's own language for the first time.

In conversations with people, there have been many moments when the other person seemed to understand without being told. Some of them were possible not because the explanation was good, but because the listener knew more of the context or interpreted the words more generously. When such help is taken for granted, it is difficult to notice how many blanks one's language contains. AI does not kindly point out those blanks. Instead, it presents a result in which they have been filled with a different meaning.

## Learning each other's dialect {#learning-dialects}

Using AI well is not a matter of discovering a perfect command that works everywhere. It is closer to observing what results our words produce, checking how the AI receives them, and gradually aligning two different interpretations.

Through conversation, users learn which expressions the AI interprets broadly, which premises it easily misses, and what must be stated more concretely. The way they describe a desired result is not completed in one attempt. They change an expression after seeing the result, compare what follows, and gradually build a language that works between them.

At the same time, users discover their own dialect. They encounter words they had been using vaguely, criteria they had never stated, and gaps they expected someone else to fill. A result that initially felt wrong in some indistinct way becomes explainable after several comparisons. While explaining an intention to AI, users may only then understand what they truly wanted.

A prompt is less a final document into which a finished intention is entered than a provisional translation that is continuously revised between different languages. What matters is not the ability to speak perfectly on the first attempt, but the ability to see the difference between interpretations in the result and carry that discovery into the next exchange.

The imperfection of language is not a concept created by the arrival of AI. The gaps in language that people and communities once filled quietly have simply become more visible before an unfamiliar counterpart.

What we learn by talking with AI is not only the language AI uses. We also learn our own language, which had been too familiar for us to see.
